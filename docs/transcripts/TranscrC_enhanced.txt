<p>Lorenze confirmed that he has not spoken to the victim, his sister, in 30 years and said he has respected her wishes by not initiating contact or forcing an apology on her, recognizing that accepting an apology should not be her responsibility and that any such interaction must occur only on her terms. He acknowledged the profound harm his actions may have caused, including lasting effects on trust, emotional security, and mental health, and the panel noted the significance of his understanding that forgiveness is not something he is owed. Lorenze also said that, while tentative, he hopes to pursue law school, believing his experience with the legal system and empathy for those involved in it could be valuable. The Office of Victim Services then explained that although it had spoken with the victim that morning and she wished to participate, her statement had not been sent directly to the office; instead, it had apparently been routed through family members and provided to Lorenze’s attorney. After discussion, the panel agreed to allow the attorney to read the statement, relying on her representation that it was authentic. In the letter, the victim stated that she had not seen or spoken to Lorenze in three decades but had considered the matter carefully, including speaking with their mother, and concluded that a pardon would not pose a danger to herself or others. She emphasized that her recommendation was not based on family reconciliation or idealized notions of forgiveness, but on her assessment of Lorenze’s current ability to function and the role that decades of legal scrutiny had played in preventing further harm. She therefore personally recommended the pardon. The chair acknowledged the statement as an act of grace, and Lorenze affirmed that he understood and appreciated the limits and conditions under which it was offered; the panel members raised no objections.</p>
